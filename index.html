Origins & The Platform Landscape

Where tokenized stocks came from, and who the major issuers are today

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the three historical "eras" of tokenized stock platforms
  • Explain what made Ondo's "just-in-time liquidity" model different
  • Distinguish between a public-stock token and a pre-IPO token
  • Describe how Robinhood Chain and Coinbase's stock tokens are structured

1.1 Era One — Ondo Finance

Ondo Stocks (originally Ondo Global Markets) became the early reference point for tokenized equities because every token is fully backed by real shares and ETFs held with U.S. broker-dealers or trust companies. Its defining feature is just-in-time liquidity: instead of relying on a thin, pre-funded pool of tokens, minting and redemption taps directly into real Nasdaq and NYSE liquidity at the moment of the trade. This cut execution slippage on large trades from roughly 200 basis points at some early competitors down to around 2 basis points on Ondo.

Just-in-Time Liquidity

A minting model that sources real exchange liquidity at the moment of a trade, rather than relying on a fixed pool that can run thin during high demand.

Ondo also built in protections most early platforms skipped: an independent Verification Agent reviews asset backing daily, and a Security Agent holds a legal, first-priority claim on the underlying assets for tokenholders' benefit.

1.2 Era Two — The Solana Wave

xStocks brought tokenized public equities onto Solana, giving Solana-native users direct exposure without leaving their existing ecosystem. PreStocks went further, tokenizing exposure to private, pre-IPO companies like OpenAI and Anthropic through SPV-backed tokens — access that has no traditional-brokerage equivalent at all, since retail investors cannot buy shares of a private company through a normal account.

SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle)

A legal entity created to hold a specific asset — in this case, shares of a private company — so that economic exposure to that asset can be issued as a token without transferring the company's actual equity.

Critically, a PreStocks token gives economic exposure only — no ownership, voting, or information rights, and no guaranteed liquidity — a materially different risk profile from a fully-backed public-stock token.

1.3 Era Three — Robinhood Chain

Robinhood Chain is a blockchain built specifically around tokenized real-world assets. Its Stock Tokens are standard ERC-20 contracts with a live Chainlink price feed built in, meaning any developer can build lending markets or trading venues on top of them with no special integration work. There are two separate access windows to understand:

WindowWhat It Covers
Tokenization WindowMint/burn of new tokens: Monday 02:00 CET – Saturday 02:00 CET
On-Chain TradingBuying/selling existing tokens: unrestricted, 24/7

Corporate actions (splits, dividends) are handled through an on-chain multiplier defined by ERC-8056, so a holder's raw token balance stays the same while the oracle folds the adjustment into the reported price automatically.

1.4 The Newest Entrant — Coinbase

In August 2026, Coinbase began issuing its own tokenized stocks on Base — self-custodied, 1:1-backed tokens such as AAPLc, NVDAc, GOOGLc, METAc, and SPCXc, issued under Regulation S. This is the same underlying concept as xStocks and Robinhood's Stock Tokens, from a major exchange as the issuer, and it's already producing real lending activity, covered in Chapter 5.

Key Takeaways

  • Tokenized stocks evolved in three overlapping waves: Ondo's brokerage-grade backing, the Solana wave's new access categories (PreStocks), and Robinhood Chain's purpose-built infrastructure — followed by Coinbase's own entrant.
  • "Just-in-time liquidity" is what let Ondo cut execution costs roughly a hundredfold versus pool-based competitors.
  • A pre-IPO token (PreStocks) and a public-stock token (Ondo, xStocks, Robinhood, Coinbase) carry fundamentally different risk profiles — know which one you're holding.
  • Robinhood Chain's tokenization window (mint/burn) and its trading window (buy/sell) are not the same thing — only the former ever pauses.

The Global Regulatory Shift

How access to tokenized stocks is expanding worldwide, region by region

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what the SEC's September 2026 Innovation Exemption created
  • List the core conditions attached to a Tokenized Securities Venue (TSV)
  • Place the U.S. move in the context of a broader, multi-region trend

2.1 What Changed

On September 17, 2026, the SEC issued Order 34-106402 — the "Innovation Exemption" — creating a new category called Tokenized Securities Venues (TSVs). A qualifying venue can run permissioned automated-market-maker liquidity pools trading tokenized versions of real, listed U.S. stocks without registering as a full securities exchange. It followed a string of earlier steps: a DTCC no-action letter permitting tokenization of Russell 1000 stocks and major ETFs, a joint SEC staff statement, and rule changes at both Nasdaq and the NYSE supporting tokenized share trading.

2.2 The Conditions

The exemption is a real framework, not an open door. Any stock token traded on a TSV must precisely mirror the rights of the underlying security — including dividends and voting — where many existing tokenized-stock products offer price exposure only. Purely synthetic look-alike products are not permitted, and the order itself is explicitly temporary: a five-year window the SEC is using to gather data before deciding what, if anything, becomes permanent.

2.3 Part Of A Bigger Map

This is one region's step in a global pattern, not an isolated event. The UK and South Korea are both moving on their own tokenized-securities frameworks, and Ondo Stocks already operates under an EU-approved base prospectus covering much of the EEA. The platforms in Chapter 1 already built a global access story without this exemption — what it adds is depth, plugging one of the world's largest equity markets into that same access layer rather than sitting outside it.

Key Takeaways

  • A TSV is a new, temporary (five-year) U.S. legal category — not a permanent rule, and not automatic approval for every existing platform.
  • Tokens traded on a TSV must carry real dividend and voting rights — synthetic price-trackers don't qualify.
  • The U.S. move sits inside a broader global trend of regulators building formal frameworks around tokenized equities.

Liquidity Provision vs. Holding

Why supplying liquidity changes the math — and what it costs you

Learning Objectives

  • Compare the return sources available to a holder versus a liquidity provider
  • Define impermanent loss and identify when it's most and least severe
  • Explain why fee income is not "free money"

3.1 Two Ways To Hold The Same Belief

A traditional holder of tokenized AAPL earns price appreciation, plus a dividend where applicable — that's the entire toolkit. A liquidity provider who pairs that same tokenized AAPL into a pool earns the same price exposure plus a share of every trade that swaps through that pool, continuously, independent of whether the price moves in a given hour.

3.2 The Trade-Off: Impermanent Loss

Impermanent Loss

The value gap that can open between holding two pooled assets versus holding them separately, caused by the pool automatically rebalancing its holdings as the assets' relative prices move.

Impermanent loss bites hardest on volatile pairs, sharp one-directional price moves, and wide divergence between the pooled assets over the holding period. It's minimal on tighter, well-managed ranges and pairs that move together rather than apart. This is why range selection and active position management are the real skill in stock-LP investing — not simply depositing capital and walking away.

Key Takeaways

  • LP income is compensation for a real risk (impermanent loss), not a free bonus on top of holding.
  • A holder's return sources: price appreciation + dividends. An LP's: price appreciation + dividends + fee income, minus impermanent-loss exposure.
  • Range width is the main lever a liquidity provider controls to manage that trade-off.

The Weekend Edge

Why on-chain pools keep earning through hours the stock market is closed

Learning Objectives

  • Calculate what share of a week traditional markets are open versus closed
  • Explain why this creates a fee-earning opportunity for LP positions
  • Identify the risk that comes with that same closed window

4.1 The Math

There are 168 hours in a week. The U.S. stock market is open roughly 32.5 of them — 6.5 trading hours a day, five days a week — which is about 19% of the week. That leaves roughly 135.5 hours, or about 81% of every week, when the underlying market is closed but a tokenized stock pool keeps trading and keeps earning fees. The single largest stretch inside that closed time is the weekend itself: the roughly 65.5 hours from Friday's close to Monday's open make up about 39% of the entire week, on their own.

4.2 The Opportunity — And The Catch

A tokenized blue-chip pool can keep earning fees through Friday night, the weekend, and Monday pre-market — hours a traditional brokerage account earns nothing beyond a static price. But because the underlying stock isn't trading, weekend price discovery can be thinner, and gaps can be sharper once markets reopen Monday — especially around earnings or major news that lands after Friday's close. A position ranged tightly for weekend fee capture is also the position most exposed to a real Monday gap.

Key Takeaways

  • The market is open only about 19% of the week and closed about 81% — and a tokenized pool earns fees through all of that closed time too.
  • The weekend alone (Friday close to Monday open) is about 39% of the entire week, on its own.
  • Tighter weekend ranges capture more fee share but carry more Monday-gap exposure — it's a trade-off, not a free edge.

Borrowing Without Selling

Ripe Finance, Kamino, and Morpho — three ways to turn a stock token into liquid capital

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how collateralized borrowing differs from selling or TradFi margin
  • Describe how Ripe Finance's multi-collateral model works
  • Describe Kamino's isolated xStocks Market and why it's isolated
  • Describe Morpho's Coinbase stock-token markets and how curators set terms

5.1 Why Borrow Instead Of Sell

Selling a stock position to raise cash gives up all future upside on what you sold. A TradFi margin loan requires broker approval and credit-based underwriting. An on-chain collateralized loan is permissionless — deposit the token, borrow against it at a set loan-to-value (LTV) ratio, keep full price exposure. The trade-off: if the collateral's value falls far enough, the position can be automatically liquidated to repay the loan — the same core risk as margin, enforced by code instead of a broker's desk.

Loan-to-Value (LTV)

The percentage of your collateral's value you're allowed to borrow — a 70% LTV on $10,000 of collateral means you can borrow up to $7,000.

5.2 Ripe Finance

Ripe lets you deposit a whole portfolio — ETH, stablecoins, Robinhood Stock Tokens — into one combined position and borrow a single stablecoin, GREEN, against it at weighted terms. Accepted stock tokens (NVDA, AAPL, TSLA, SPCX, GOOGL, GME) carry a flat 70% LTV and a 7% borrow rate, plus a roughly 0.25% origination fee. Ripe is still early-stage — public trackers have shown thin total value locked, meaning less battle-testing and thinner liquidity than a fully established protocol.

5.3 Kamino

Kamino Lend became the first major DeFi lending protocol anywhere to accept tokenized stocks as collateral, in July 2025. Its xStocks Market runs as an isolated market — meaning a problem with tokenized-stock collateral can't cascade into Kamino's much larger core crypto lending pools — and prices through Chainlink Data Streams, a sub-second feed built to handle equity-style volatility and trading-hours gaps. Kamino has held onto roughly 82.6% of all Solana tokenized-stock lending TVL since launch.

Isolated Market

A lending market walled off from a protocol's other pools, so risk in one collateral type cannot spread into unrelated deposits.

5.4 Morpho — Coinbase Stock Tokens

On September 7, 2026, Morpho opened lending markets on Base for five of Coinbase's tokenized stocks: AAPLc, GOOGLc, NVDAc, METAc, and SPCXc. Each market is independently curated (all five by Steakhouse Financial), priced by Chainlink, and borrowable in USDC at variable or fixed rates. Liquidation LTV sits at 62.5% for AAPL/NVDA/META/SPCX and 77% for GOOGL. Borrowing jumped from about $500 to roughly $42,000 in two hours on September 16 as usage picked up, and Meta's market has run hot enough to push utilization past 97% with borrow rates spiking to around 17.5% — a live example of rates responding to real-time demand.

Key Takeaways

  • Collateralized borrowing keeps your original price exposure fully intact — the loan sits alongside it, not in place of it.
  • Ripe = multi-collateral, one combined position, single borrowed stablecoin (GREEN). Still early-stage.
  • Kamino = isolated xStocks Market, dominant share of Solana tokenized-stock lending, sub-second Chainlink pricing.
  • Morpho = curator-set markets on Base for Coinbase's stock tokens; rates rise sharply as utilization climbs.

The Flywheel

How every piece compounds into the next

Learning Objectives

  • List the five steps of the flywheel in order
  • Explain what does and doesn't happen to your original position when you borrow against it

6.1 The Five Steps

StepAction
1Buy the stock token — gain price exposure, 24/7, self-custodied
2Supply it as liquidity — earn ongoing fee income on top
3Compound the fees — reinvest instead of cashing out
4Borrow against the stack — unlock capital without selling
5Redeploy the capital — new positions, the loop repeats

The critical detail: at no point does the original position get sold or reduced. A loan taken in Step 4 is additional capital working alongside the original exposure, not a replacement for it. That's the structural difference between this approach and a traditional shareholder's single lever — hope the price goes up.

Key Takeaways

  • The flywheel loops: Step 5 feeds back into Step 1.
  • Borrowing against a position never removes that position's price exposure.
